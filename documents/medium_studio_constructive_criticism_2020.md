# 6 Reasons Why the Studio Program at Cornell Tech Succeeded in Deserving Some Constructive Criticism

Author: Yaniv Ravid

Published: Jun 29, 2020

The Cornell Tech community can agree two things when it comes to their least favorite aspects of working at and going to a school on Roosevelt Island: there is no good food and the F line is never on time. Obviously, personal opinions and biases will affect this list if people are individually asked to list what they don’t like about Cornell Tech.

At least from my experience, I found that students’ answers will have more in common than they differ, and one the more frequent common denominators that the answers will have will include this word: Studio. The Studio program at Cornell Tech has a clear mission: build the next Silicon Valley in New York City. It’s a noble cause that should be venerated by the entire Cornell Tech community if not the entire NYC Venture Capital world itself. However, the Studio program fails to do this in many ways.

I will only list six:

1. Starting a company and building a business is more of a life choice than it is a career. Due to this reason, the concepts, values and practices that go into developing a product or leading a firm are some of the most valuable life lessons and skills anyone could pick up. It is a shame that the Studio team does not take this seriously enough. Even though it teaches students the importance of research, ideation and testing, the Studio team treats them as nothing more than a soft skill.

As a student, it is really difficult to respect and fully internalize subjects and topics if the faculty that is teaching it does not take it seriously in the first place.

2. Similar to the point above, the faculty lacks the passion about their teaching. Again, this lack of passion quickly mirrors itself in the students, which begin to treat the classes the same way the faculty does: go through the motions, do the bare minimum.

While the mission of the program continues to push for a new Silicon Valley in NYC, and students gets asked serious questions such as “what do you want to do with the next five years of your life?”, guiding them in the right way to go about building a business, what should be a process of constantly looking far into the future becomes a daily routine of finding the earliest possible time I could get off the island or back to The House.

What should be a passion that pushes students toward a launch, ends up being mostly faculty complaining about lunch.

3. This lack of passion can be explained by an easy browse through faculty members’ credentials: most have worked with each other in the past. A good portion of the Studio team worked at the same company together at one point in their life. This lack of professional, as well as general, diversity in the Studio team hints of blatant nepotism. To add fuel to the fire, many of the team members do not show mastery of the subject areas in which they teach.

4. Many of these members have been teaching at Cornell Tech for years. The fact that someone with clear lack of experience in teaching as well as the area in which they teach, who is not passionate about Cornell Tech’s mission, made me question how can such an individual maintain their position.

5. Along with the Studio team, guest speakers play a main role in the Studio experience. It is frequent to see the same guest speaker appear in several classes, making the experience repetitive and wasteful of everybody’s time. At occasions, guest speakers’ give presentations with very little (and sometimes even irrelevant) substance. This was proven to me in the Spring semester, when more than half of the Studio students decided to skip a guest lecture.

The effort and passion students saw from the Studio team was reflected perfectly that day.

6. Far above all of the previous points, the most important feedback I could give the Studio team has to do with standards. The standards to which the Studio teams holds the Studio program were obvious to me during the year, and by obvious I mean almost nonexistent. On the other hand, the standards to which the students were held were unclear at best. Ambiguity obscured the experience completely.

After hours of effort and adherence to the curriculum were put in by student teams, many found themselves held to opinionated and subjective grading policies that seemed more unjustified than the F train during rush hour. Having the final verdict of abundant work be subject to such academic injustice seems worse than any other misdemeanor the Studio team might be guilty of.

This might be no panegyric, but my Cornell Tech experience was overwhelmingly positive. It generated great respect in me toward Cornell Tech and its mission and faculty. My Studio experience in itself was ridden with dissonance and afflictions, but my decision to write this was influenced by someone else’s experiences rather than mine.

It wasn’t until I heard a fellow student tell me they are looking at other programs, because a Cornell Tech diploma was “a throwaway degree,” that I decided to convey my feelings in this formal way.
